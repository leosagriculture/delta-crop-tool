How to use the Delta Crop Costing Tool

Every business runs on one rule: you have to take in more than you spend. Sales minus costs is profit. This tool helps you see that rule crop by crop, so you can put your beds, hours and money into the crops that pay their way and feed the most people.

The top-down method

  1. Find the price. Use your own past sales first. If you have none, ask another Delta grower, ask a wholesale buyer, or check a USDA Agricultural Marketing Service price report.
  2. Set a gross margin goal. Gross margin is the share of every sales dollar left after direct inputs and direct labor. For retail sales like farmers markets and farm stands, 40 to 60 percent is a good target. For wholesale, 20 percent is a good target and hard to reach.
  3. Enter your crop costs. Direct inputs like seed, compost and packaging, and every hour it takes to grow, harvest, wash, pack and sell the crop.
  4. Check the result. Did the crop reach your goal? If not, the tool shows the price that would get you there.

Two kinds of cost

  • Operating or direct costs go up and down with how much you grow: seed, fertilizer, packaging, and the labor to grow, harvest, wash and pack. These go in each crop budget.
  • Overhead or fixed costs stay the same no matter what you plant: plot fees, insurance, tool repairs, fencing, fuel to market. These go in Garden setup. Your gross profit from all crops has to cover them, plus any pay for the grower, before the garden makes a true profit.

Three levers for more income

  • Sell all you have. Losses before harvest come from planting too much, weeds, insects and disease, weather, and not enough hands to pick. Losses after harvest come from no market, produce wilting on the table, and no cooler. In the Delta heat, after-harvest losses climb fast in July and August.
  • Grow more, or get better yield from the beds you have. Only worth it if the crop already makes money on every unit.
  • Change your price. A small price change makes a big difference in gross profit. If your break-even price is above what you charge, growing more only loses more.

Reading the Whole garden rankings

  • Net profit % compares what a crop earns to what it costs, after its share of overhead. It shows which crops use your money best.
  • Total net profit is the dollars a crop brings home. A crop high on this list also has customers who want a lot of it.
  • Gross profit is what is left after production costs. It shows your growing skill with that crop.

Delta notes

  • Much of the Delta sits on heavy buckshot clay and silty loam. Get a soil test through the MSU Extension Soil Testing Lab before buying lime or fertilizer.
  • Planting windows shown are typical for the Delta. Confirm dates with the MSU Extension garden planting guide and the frost forecast.
  • Starter prices are rough Delta farmers market figures for 2026. Replace them with what your customers at the Greenville, Cleveland and Vicksburg markets actually pay.
  • Community food value counts the produce you donate, share or take home at market price. It is part of the garden's return even when no money changes hands.

For Master Gardener classes

Have each trainee budget the same crop in the same bed, then compare. Differences in hours, losses and prices start the best conversations about how a garden really runs.

A word of caution

This tool is only as good as the numbers you put in. It assumes your gross margin goal is realistic and that your prices and yields come from real records. Keep a harvest log and a time log this season, and next year's budgets will tell you the truth.
